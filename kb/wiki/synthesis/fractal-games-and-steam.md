# Build the game layer, skip the engine

almondbread can become a game, but only as a thin, rules-driven layer over the renderer it already has, and the Steam edition only pays if it is sold as a game rather than a tool. The evidence ranks the candidate mechanics clearly: a **daily "find this place" challenge** and a **shared atlas where the first discoverer names a structure** have the strongest support; **depth missions** supply the goals that exploration games are punished for lacking; **journey mode** works as a mode but fails as a product; **canyon racing** is the most expensive and least proven. No commercial deep-zoom Mandelbrot game exists. The only fractal game with traction, Fractal Block World, has **1,652 Steam reviews at 98% positive** and earned them through mystery and secrets, while fractal *tools* on Steam stall at 20 to 30 reviews ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1540570)). Steam itself is cheap to enter ($100 per app) and Steamworks covers leaderboards, ghost replays, cloud saves and clips. It does not cover a trustworthy "first discoverer" claim, which needs a small backend of almondbread's own. On the technical side, **no game engine is needed or helpful**: Unity, Unreal, Godot and Bevy would all force the tuned compute renderer to be re-hosted inside someone else's frame loop. The right stack is a Rust desktop app on **wgpu + winit** with a WGSL port of the roughly 535-line kernel set, **steamworks-rs** behind a feature flag, and the existing Metal backend kept for Apple. Realistic Steam revenue is in the low thousands to low tens of thousands of dollars in the first week, so the plan below spends the cheap, reusable work first (daily challenge on Apple, wgpu backend behind the CLI) and only commits to the full Steam build once a Coming Soon page shows wishlist velocity.

## One fractal game succeeded, and it did so with secrets, not depth

The fractal niche on Steam is small and lopsided. Fractal Block World (FBW, $6.99, Windows, released October 2021) is the only fractal-world game with real traction: **1,652 reviews, 98.2% positive**, which SteamSpy translates into **50,000 to 100,000 owners**, with a peak of 72 concurrent players on its snapshot day ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1540570); [SteamSpy](https://steamspy.com/app/1540570)). It is a "surreal exploration game" in which "you can shrink infinitely to go deeper into the fractal". Its reviewers praise 16 to 50+ hours of play, an ending, repeat runs and secrets "I might die before I find it all" ([FBW reviews](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/1540570?json=1&language=english&review_type=positive)). It also grew out of a codebase that dates back to about 2008, so its success came after roughly a decade of work ([nextquest.dev](https://nextquest.dev/games/infinifrag-2)).

Everything else in the fractal space is a tool or a free toy, and the numbers show it. Fractal Alchemist ($19.99) has **29 reviews** and MandelCraft (free, $24.99 DLC) has **20**, both tagged as design tools ([Fractal Alchemist](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1318030); [MandelCraft](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4081860)). Fractal Fly, a free 3D fractal flyer, sits at 78.8% on 151 reviews. Players call it "VERY outdated", say "flying is janky and the details don't go small enough", and one "got a little sick from playing this" ([Fractal Fly reviews](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/1860120?json=1&language=english)). Those complaints land exactly where almondbread is strong (stable deep detail) and exactly where landscape mode is risky (flight feel, motion sickness). Marble Marcher, CodeParade's ray-marched marble race over fractal surfaces, rated 4.7/5 on itch.io but stayed free and is no longer developed ([itch.io](https://codeparade.itch.io/marblemarcher)). The nearest thing to almondbread's daily-challenge idea is **MandelMatcher**, a free 2025 game-jam entry ("Explore the Mandelbrot set to find the pattern you see on the screen!") with no daily cadence, no scoring and no share card ([itch.io](https://radian628.itch.io/mandelmatcher)). A Steam search for "mandelbrot" returns nothing. The only upcoming Mandelbrot title, RealMandelRender ("132 curated destinations", image and video export, 5 November 2026), is a viewer and a direct competitor to a tool-shaped Steam release, not a game ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5227800)).

The adjacent maths and scale games mark out the ceiling and the trap. Where the maths drives puzzles, ratings and sales are high: Superliminal has **34,364 reviews at 94.6%** and Manifold Garden **8,617 at 95.2%** ([Superliminal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1049410); [Manifold Garden](https://store.steampowered.com/app/473950)). Where the maths is mostly the spectacle, ratings drop. Hyperbolica sits at 83% with "View+idea was nice, game was boring", and Everything also sits at 83% with "overstays its welcome" and "not a game" ([Hyperbolica reviews](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/1256230?json=1&language=english); [Everything reviews](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/582270?json=1&language=english)). Proteus is at 76%: "not much to see or do... about an hour" ([Proteus reviews](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/219680?json=1&language=english)). The mystery-driven Outer Wilds holds **95.6% on 109,245 reviews** ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/753640)). In the table below, owner estimates are Steam-only and use the notes' rough rule of 30 to 60 owners per review, so treat them as orders of magnitude.

| Game | Core idea | Steam reviews, % positive | Rough owners | Lesson for almondbread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fractal Block World | Shrink infinitely through a fractal world; lore, ending | 1,652, 98% | 50k–100k (SteamSpy) | Fractal plus mystery and goals works |
| Fractal Alchemist / MandelCraft | Fractal creation tools | 29 / 20 | hundreds | Tools do not sell on Steam |
| Fractal Fly | Free 3D fractal flight | 151, 79% | free | Janky flight and nausea get punished |
| Marble Marcher | Marble time trials on fractals | itch 4.7/5 (167) | free | Fractal racing exists only as a free demo |
| MandelMatcher | Find the shown Mandelbrot pattern | jam game | free | Daily-challenge idea untested at scale |
| Superliminal / Manifold Garden | Maths drives puzzles | 34k, 95% / 8.6k, 95% | ~1M+ / ~260k+ | Puzzles make maths a game |
| Hyperbolica / Everything / Proteus | Maths or scale as spectacle | 1.3k / 3.9k / 2.8k, 76–83% | 40k–230k | "Not a game", "too short" |

Two conclusions follow. First, the open slot is real: nobody has shipped a game built on deep-zoom 2D Mandelbrot exploration, and almondbread's exact, shared, infinitely deep coordinate space is a world no competitor has turned into play. Second, the slot is unvalidated. No postmortems exist because nobody has tried and reported back, and the closest success needed authored mystery on top of the fractal.

## Daily challenge and the atlas lead; racing has the weakest case for its cost

The **daily challenge** has the best ratio of evidence to cost. Wordle grew from **90 players to about 300,000 in two months** on the back of a spoiler-free share grid, and the NYT bought it for "low-seven figures" ([WHYY/NPR](https://whyy.org/npr-story/the-new-york-times-buys-wordle)). GeoGuessr, which reportedly has 80M+ players, moved to a full subscription in 2024 but **kept the daily challenge free as its funnel** ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/companies/geoguessr/)). The wave also decays: Heardle fell from about 69M to 41M monthly visits and was shut down in 2023 ([dot.la](https://dot.la/spotify-heardle-shut-down-2659860389.html)). For almondbread, that makes the daily challenge an acquisition and retention feature, not the product. It is cheap because it reuses exact share links and the deterministic renderer. MandelMatcher shows the core loop is playable but omitted the two parts that made Wordle spread, the daily cadence and the share card.

The **discovery atlas** is the most distinctive hook and the one with a sting in the tail. Permanent, public credit motivates people strongly. Hanny van Arkel, a schoolteacher volunteering for Galaxy Zoo, found "Hanny's Voorwerp", which now carries her name, and she co-authored the 2009 paper ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanny%27s_Voorwerp)). Foldit player groups are listed as co-authors of a 2011 *Nature Structural & Molecular Biology* paper ([UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2011/09/19/gamers-succeed-where-scientists-fail)). No Man's Sky shows the cost: Hello Games told Kotaku it is "common sadly for the most problematic players to find ways to have the most offensive names" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/no-mans-sky-galactic-hub-bases-civilized-space-neo-nazi-1848739563)), and named discoveries appearing to vanish caused a launch-week scare ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/96530/no-mans-sky-appears-to-delete-your-discoveries-after-two-weeks-updated)). The atlas therefore needs a name filter, a report path and admin renames from day one. It also needs a classification that stops near-duplicate "discoveries" from flooding the atlas: canonical identity by period and nucleus position from [[techniques/reference-selection]] is the natural key. almondbread's exact coordinates make claims unusually verifiable, because a server can recompute them.

**Depth missions** ("reach a period-1000 minibrot in three minutes", "pass 1e100 without touching the interior") have indirect but consistent support. The exploration games that sell attach goals, an ending or secrets (FBW, Outer Wilds), and the goal-less ones collect "not a game" and length complaints. Gone Home drew "a massive backlash from consumers who dismissed it as a game entirely" at $20 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gone_Home)). Missions are also cheap content built from the same verification as the atlas, and they matter for a commercial reason specific to Steam: refunds are granted within 14 days for anything played under two hours ([Steam Refunds](https://store.steampowered.com/steam_refunds/)), so a contemplative explorer that feels "done" in 90 minutes is a refund risk.

**Journey mode** is safe as one mode among several and weak as a product. It reuses the autopilot, and it serves the relaxation audience the market research identified. Sold alone, it inherits Proteus's and Everything's reviews.

**Canyon racing** has the weakest evidence relative to its cost. The only fractal time trial, Marble Marcher, stayed free and was abandoned. Fractal flight draws janky-controls and nausea complaints. Racing also depends on landscape mode ([[decisions/landscape-mode]]), which is itself an unbuilt prototype with an open question on the cost of near rings at depth. Steamworks does support the racing pattern well (ghosts attached to leaderboard entries, below), so racing is technically cheap *once landscape mode exists*. It should be a post-launch update gated on a flight-feel and motion-sickness playtest, not a launch pillar.

| Rank | Mechanic | Evidence | Build cost | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Daily challenge | Strong (Wordle, GeoGuessr funnel); decays | Low: share links, one seed per day, leaderboard | Build first, ship on Apple too |
| 2 | Atlas, discoverer naming | Strong motivator; moderation burden (NMS) | Medium: own backend, classification, moderation | Core Steam hook |
| 3 | Depth missions | Indirect but consistent (goals sell) | Low–medium: reuses atlas verification | Launch content; counters refunds |
| 4 | Journey mode | Weak alone ("not a game") | Low: autopilot exists | Include as a mode, never market it as the game |
| 5 | Canyon racing | Weakest; one free precedent; nausea risk | High: needs landscape mode | Post-launch update, behind a prototype gate |

## Steam costs $100 and six weeks; wishlists decide the outcome

The mechanics of getting onto Steam are cheap and slow rather than expensive. Onboarding means signing the distribution agreement, paying a **$100 fee per app** (recouped once the app reaches $1,000 adjusted gross revenue) and passing bank, tax (W-8BEN for a non-US developer) and identity checks that "may take 10 to 15 business days" ([Steamworks Onboarding](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/onboarding); [App Fee](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee)). The onboarding page today states a **21-day wait** between paying the fee and release, but another summary of the same page said 30 days, so plan for 30. A public Coming Soon page must be live for at least two weeks, and Valve reviews the store page and the build separately, each in one to five days ([Onboarding](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/onboarding)). Valve keeps **30% up to $10M**, which is the only tier that matters here ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1753729)). Builds go up through SteamPipe, with one depot per OS and password-protected beta branches for testers ([Uploading](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/sdk/uploading)). The minimum calendar is six to eight weeks from signup to release. The marketing calendar is far longer.

Marketing is the real constraint. GameDiscoverCo puts median first-week sales at **0.15× wishlists** for games launching with more than 25k wishlists, and **0.10× for games priced above $10**. Conversion "varies by an order of magnitude", and underperformers had a median 7-day user score of 67% against 91% for overperformers ([GameDiscoverCo via GameDevReports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gamediscoverco-the-state-of-steam)). How To Market A Game's benchmarks put the **Bronze tier (about $1,000 first-week revenue) at about 5,000 wishlists** at launch and Silver at 8,000. Organic wishlist velocity of 0 to 40 a week is Bronze territory and 15 to 120 a week is Silver ([HTMAG Benchmarks](https://howtomarketagame.com/benchmarks/)). The median Steam game earns somewhere between about $249 a year and about $1,000 lifetime, depending on which secondary aggregation you believe, and nearly half of 2025's roughly 20,000 releases never reached 10 reviews ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/median-steam-game-2026)). As an illustration, not a forecast: 5,000 wishlists × 0.15 × $9.99 gives about 750 first-week units and roughly $7,500 gross, or about $5,000 after Valve's cut, before VAT and refunds. That figure sits in the same range as the market research's first-year Apple scenario, so Steam roughly doubles a small number rather than changing its order of magnitude.

Next Fest is the one big free visibility event, and it can be spent only once: "Titles are only allowed to participate in one Next Fest". Entry needs a public store page and a playable demo, and the game must release after the fest ([Steamworks Next Fest](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest/2026june)). The February 2027 edition reportedly closes registration on 10 January 2027 ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/steam-next-fest-february-2027)), which is too early for a ported renderer plus a game layer. A later 2027 edition is realistic. The ideal demo is the daily challenge plus a handful of depth missions, which is small, replayable and shows the hook in minutes. The release should be classified as a **Game**, not Software. That is an inference, because no Valve rule spelling out the difference was found, but the Software category sits outside the Next Fest and tag-driven discovery culture that a niche title depends on.

### Steamworks covers leaderboards and ghosts, but not first-discoverer claims

Steamworks fits the planned modes closely. Leaderboards allow **up to 10,000 per app**, one entry per player, an int32 score plus **64 int32 "details"**, and KeepBest or ForceUpdate uploads. `AttachLeaderboardUGC` links a shared file "such as a replay or a ghost" to an entry ([Steamworks Leaderboards](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/leaderboards)). A daily challenge becomes one board per day (the seed and version go in the details; 10,000 boards is about 27 years), and racing becomes one board per track with flight-recorder ghosts attached. Steam Cloud syncs bookmarks and flights across OSes through the API or no-code Auto-Cloud ([Steam Cloud](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/cloud)). Workshop can host locations, flights and palettes ([Workshop](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/workshop)). Timeline markers (`AddInstantaneousTimelineEvent` for "new depth record", "discovery claimed") give players clip-ready moments with almost no code ([Timeline](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/timeline)), which suits a visual product that spreads through clips.

Two gaps need almondbread's own server. Client-written leaderboards can be forged, and **Trusted** boards accept scores only through the `SetLeaderboardScore` Web API, which needs a publisher key on a server ([Leaderboards](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/leaderboards)). More importantly, nothing in Steam provides an atomic, timestamped "first claim wins" record, so the atlas needs a small backend: a serverless function plus a database, authenticating Steam users with session tickets and recomputing each claimed location on the CPU before accepting it. One backend should serve both stores, so the atlas is a single world across App Store and Steam players. That backend processes Steam IDs and probably IP addresses, so it brings GDPR, a privacy policy and a retention statement with it. Achievements, Rich Presence ("Exploring Seahorse Valley at 1e-42") and Steam Input action sets complete the picture. Trading cards are not available at launch.

### Steam Deck matters more than the Mac build

The September 2026 Steam survey reports **Windows 95.03%, Linux 3.05%, macOS 1.92%** ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/10/steam-survey-for-september-2026-shows-a-drop-in-linux-with-simplified-chinese-rising/)). The Mac build is a rounding error on Steam, and the Deck is the platform worth designing for. Deck Verified comes down to defaults: full controller support with no in-game configuration, glyphs that match the device, the on-screen keyboard for text entry, **text at least 9 px at 1280×800**, 30 fps at 800p and no launcher. Valve tests a native Linux build first and falls back to the Windows build under Proton ([Steam Deck Compatibility](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/steamdeck/compat)). Fractal navigation maps naturally onto sticks and triggers. The risk is GPU load: deep-zoom compute at 800p on the Deck's integrated AMD GPU is unmeasured, and the scheduler's adaptive chunk budget is the mechanism that has to absorb it.

macOS on Steam has a hard rule: Steam "is not currently compatible with the com.apple.security.app-sandbox entitlement", and builds must be 64-bit, notarized and allow unsigned library loading for the overlay ([Steamworks Platforms](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/application/platforms)). The Mac App Store requires the sandbox, so **the Steam Mac build and the App Store build are necessarily different binaries**, and Steamworks features do not exist in the App Store build.

## Keep the renderer, add wgpu and a Rust shell, and leave engines alone

The case against a game engine comes down to architecture, not licensing. Licensing is now benign for an indie: Unity cancelled the Runtime Fee and Personal is free under $200k ([Unity](https://unity.com/blog/unity-is-canceling-the-runtime-fee)), and Unreal charges 5% only above $1M lifetime gross ([Unreal FAQ](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/faq)). The problem is that almondbread's product *is* a compute renderer. The `iterate` kernel relies on SIMD-group prefix sums and threadgroup atomics for order-preserving live-list compaction (per-SIMD-group atomics measured 44% slower), on indirect dispatch, on per-submission GPU timestamps that feed the throughput model, and on 16 ms chunk budgets under the GPU watchdog ([[platform/render-architecture]], [[platform/gpu-watchdog-and-chunking]]). Each engine either hides those controls or exposes them through a second-class path.

| Option | What happens to the renderer | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Unity / Unreal + Rust native plugin | Black box owning its own GPU device; texture sharing with the engine swapchain is awkward; 100–200 MB runtime; C#/C++ added | No: engine reduced to a menu shell |
| Godot 4 + godot-rust | Kernels rewritten in GLSL for RenderingDevice, manual RID lifetimes, TDR splitting by hand, no RenderDoc for local devices ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/shaders/compute_shaders.html)); binding at 0.5.5 ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/godot)) | No: worst of both |
| Bevy 0.20 | Same language and wgpu underneath, but seven breaking releases in 27 months, and 0.20 forces a shader-dialect migration (WGSL to WESL) ([Bevy 0.20](https://bevy.org/news/bevy-0-20/)) | Only `bevy_ecs`, and only if racing grows many entities |
| Fyrox 1.0 | Compute support not researched | Not evaluated |
| Custom wgpu + winit | `GpuBackend` implemented on wgpu; scheduler unchanged | **Yes** |

Tiny Glade, the best-known Rust game, was reportedly built on Bevy's ECS with its own Vulkan renderer rather than Bevy's renderer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiny_Glade); [GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2024/09/chill-diorama-builder-tiny-glade-is-out-now/comment_id=269684)). The detail is secondary-sourced, but it points the same way: keep your own renderer and borrow only what you need. almondbread's game state (a camera, a few rules, a leaderboard, some ghosts) needs no ECS at all.

**wgpu covers every feature the backend trait uses.** wgpu 30 (July 2026; 30.0.1 in August) provides subgroup operations on Vulkan, DX12 and Metal, indirect dispatch (`dispatch_workgroups_indirect` since v29), pass-level timestamp queries, immediates (formerly push constants), pipeline-overridable constants for the `kDE`/`kInterior` specialisation, and stable passthrough shaders as an escape hatch ([wgpu CHANGELOG](https://github.com/gfx-rs/wgpu/blob/trunk/CHANGELOG.md); [wgpu Features](https://docs.rs/wgpu/latest/wgpu/struct.Features.html)). Vello, a production wgpu compute renderer built on hand-written WGSL prefix sums, is the precedent ([crates.io vello](https://crates.io/crates/vello)). The mapping is mechanical: `simd_prefix_exclusive_sum`/`simd_sum` become `subgroupExclusiveAdd`/`subgroupAdd`, `threadgroup` becomes `var<workgroup>`, `gpu_time()` becomes two timestamps per pass, and `present` becomes one blit from the colorised storage texture to the surface. wgpu costs four things. First, it ships about **one breaking major per quarter** (v27 to v30 in nine months), so pin a version and upgrade deliberately. Second, it has **no fast-math control** on Metal ([wgpu-hal metal/device.rs](https://github.com/gfx-rs/wgpu/blob/trunk/wgpu-hal/src/metal/device.rs)) and no bit-identical guarantee across backends, so the "bit-identical iteration fields" rule becomes a tolerance rule against the CPU oracle for non-Metal backends. Third, **subgroup size varies** (32 on NVIDIA and Apple, 32 or 64 on AMD, 8 to 32 on Intel), so the `sgLive[32]` arrays must be sized from the adapter. Fourth, a Windows TDR becomes a full device-lost event, so the backend must recreate the device rather than retry a command buffer. Raw Vulkan through `ash` is a poor alternative: `ash` has had no release since April 2024 ([crates.io ash](https://crates.io/crates/ash)), and a raw backend would double maintenance for no measured gain at three dispatches per chunk.

**Steamworks from Rust is solved.** steamworks-rs 0.13.1 (May 2026, with commits through August) tracks Steamworks SDK 1.64 and wraps UserStats (achievements, leaderboards), UGC, RemoteStorage, Input and Timeline. It loads Valve's `steam_api` library dynamically on all three OSes ([steamworks-rs](https://github.com/Noxime/steamworks-rs); [docs.rs](https://docs.rs/steamworks/latest/steamworks/)). If it stalls, it is cheap to fork, because its bindings are generated with bindgen.

### What carries over, and the crate layout that keeps two product lines apart

Most of the investment carries over unchanged. `almondbread-math` and `almondbread-core` are `forbid(unsafe_code)`, platform-free, and already `cargo check` for Linux and Windows ([[decisions/rust-core]]). The `GpuBackend` trait, the chunked scheduler, the throughput model, the preview/refine logic and the `Host<B>` frame/input loop are platform-neutral by design, and the scheduler already polls submissions and takes time as an argument, which matches a winit event loop ([[platform/render-architecture]]). The CLI's `verify`, `bench` and `regress` commands become the cross-backend test harness. The flight recorder and share-code formats planned in [[decisions/landscape-mode]] become the ghost and daily-challenge formats. Three things do not carry over: the SwiftUI views (Apple-only by design), the MSL kernels (they need a WGSL twin), and the `CAMetalLayer`-typed hosting (it needs a `Surface` trait).

```text
crates/
  almondbread-math       unchanged
  almondbread-core       unchanged; + share codes, daily seeds
  almondbread-render     GpuBackend, scheduler, Host; + Surface trait; shaders/{msl,wgsl}; metal/ (cfg apple)
  almondbread-wgpu       NEW  GpuBackend on wgpu (Vulkan/DX12), WGSL kernels, wgpu surface
  almondbread-game       NEW  pure rules, no GPU: daily seed and scoring, atlas classification
                              and naming rules, missions, race rules, ghost format; unit-tested
  almondbread-platform   NEW  traits: Achievements, Leaderboards, CloudSave, Ugc, Overlay; local impl
  almondbread-steam      NEW  platform traits on steamworks-rs, feature "steam"
  almondbread-app        NEW  winit shell, input (gamepad / Steam Input), game UI;
                              MetalBackend on macOS, WgpuBackend elsewhere
  almondbread-cli        + --backend wgpu|metal for verify/bench in CI
  almondbread-ffi        Apple only; may expose almondbread-game to SwiftUI
```

This layout gives one repository and two product lines. The App Store line is the Xcode project plus the XCFramework, sandboxed and Steam-free, as today. The Steam line is `cargo build -p almondbread-app --features steam` per OS. A DRM-free build for itch.io falls out for free with the `local` platform implementation. Putting game rules in a GPU-free `almondbread-game` crate is the key move, because it lets the **daily challenge and atlas ship in the SwiftUI app through the FFI before any port exists**, and it keeps scoring and verification identical on the client and on the atlas server. For the Steam Mac build, reuse the Rust shell with the native Metal backend rather than adding Steamworks to the SwiftUI app. That keeps one game UI, one Steam integration and one input path, and the Mac-native explorer stays on the App Store. Given macOS's 1.92% share, the Steam Mac depot can follow after Windows and Linux. The UI is the honest weak spot: egui is "very mouse-oriented", and gamepad focus navigation has come from add-on crates ([bevy_egui_kbgp](https://docs.rs/bevy-egui-kbgp)). Budget for a small custom controller-first menu layer, which Deck Verified requires anyway. CI should run a native-runner matrix: macOS for Metal tests, signing and notarization; Windows for Authenticode; and Linux inside the Steam Runtime container, plus a lavapipe software-Vulkan run of `verify` for functional checks. steamcmd then uploads to a beta branch.

## Ranked recommendation and phased plan

**Recommendation, ranked.** (1) Build the **daily challenge** first, in a platform-neutral `almondbread-game` crate, and ship it in the Apple app. It is the cheapest test of whether play on this world spreads at all. (2) Build the **wgpu backend behind the CLI** next, verified against the CPU oracle. That retires the largest technical risk for every non-Apple future, not only Steam. (3) Open a **Steam Coming Soon page early** ($100) and treat wishlist velocity as the go/no-go signal for the full Steam build. (4) Make the **atlas with discoverer naming plus depth missions** the Steam launch hook, with a small own backend and moderation from day one. (5) Include **journey mode** as a mode. (6) Defer **canyon racing** to a post-launch update behind a landscape-mode prototype and a motion-sickness playtest. (7) Use **no game engine**: wgpu + winit + steamworks-rs, with Metal kept native on Apple. This partly revises the market research's advice to cut the Windows/Linux port. The port is worth doing *as a game edition*, but only after the cheap Apple-side and CLI-side steps prove the hook and the backend.

| Phase | Work | Exit gate |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Prove the hook (on Apple) | `almondbread-game` with daily seed, scoring, share card; exposed through FFI; shipped in the SwiftUI app | Daily players return; share cards get posted |

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which engine" to "where does the game live". It lives in a small, pure Rust crate of rules that sits between a renderer almondbread already owns and storefronts it does not. Framed that way, almost every expensive-looking decision becomes cheap and reversible. The daily challenge can be tested on Apple users before Steam costs a cent, the wgpu port pays for itself by de-risking every non-Apple future, and the atlas backend serves both stores at once. The one irreversible bet, a single Next Fest slot, comes last.

The unresolved risk is not technical. It is whether deep-zoom exploration plus light rules is enough of a game when the only fractal hit needed a decade of authored mystery. The atlas is the closest thing almondbread has to authored mystery that writes itself, since players fill the world with names and the maths guarantees rarity. That makes the atlas, not racing, the feature on which the Steam edition will succeed or fail, and the one to prototype with real players as early as possible.
